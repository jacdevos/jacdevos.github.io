When I think about large language models, I keep coming back to a small black box in a children's book.

In Terry Pratchett's *Truckers*, the first novel in the *Bromeliad Trilogy*, a community of tiny people called Nomes carries an object known simply as **the Thing**. It has been handed down through generations. To them, it is an important cultural artifact, mysterious and barely understood.

Then it begins to speak.

The Thing turns out to be a computer connected to the Nomes' forgotten technological origins. It knows things they have lost, answers their questions, and helps them navigate a world far larger than they had imagined. What seems almost supernatural is, in fact, a product of their own civilisation.

That story feels newly relevant.

## Is it an alien intelligence?

We often talk about AI as if an unfamiliar intelligence has appeared from nowhere: a strange new entity that might outthink us, replace us, or threaten us. Some of those concerns deserve serious attention. But when we talk specifically about **large language models**, that framing misses something fundamental.

LLMs did not arrive from outside human culture. They were built by people and trained on enormous collections of material produced by people: writing, code, explanations, arguments, discoveries, stories, and more. Their training data is neither the whole internet nor a perfect record of human knowledge, but it represents an extraordinary breadth of human activity.

Transformer-based models learn complex relationships and patterns from that material. They do not simply store an encyclopedia and retrieve passages on demand. They learn representations that let them respond to questions, connect ideas, work through problems, and generate new material. Further training, including human feedback and other methods, shapes how they behave.

We have found a way to make a vast body of human-created knowledge **conversational—and useful**.

## A cultural artifact that talks back

Books preserve ideas. Libraries preserve collections of them. The internet lets us search and connect them.

An LLM is different. We can ask it to explain something, challenge an argument, connect two fields, or help solve a problem. It can apply patterns learned from human work to situations it has never encountered in exactly the same form.

That ability can feel uncanny. It is tempting to treat the result as an intelligence wholly separate from us.

But its origins are deeply human. Even the problem-solving behaviour we find so surprising has been shaped by learning from human examples and by training systems designed by people. That does not mean every answer is a copy of something someone wrote, or that every capability is fully understood. It means the system's capabilities have a history, and that history runs through us.

**LLMs are not alien visitors. They are technological artifacts of human civilisation that can speak back.**

## The Thing—and us

The Nomes inherited the Thing without understanding what it was. Their interpretation of it was shaped by their culture and by what they had forgotten.

We are in a different position: we know that we built our models, and we understand much of how their training works. Yet we can still be surprised by their capabilities, and we cannot always fully explain why a particular response emerges.

The comparison is not exact. The Thing is a fictional computer with a specific store of knowledge and a particular origin story. An LLM is a trained model, not a faithful archive of civilisation. It can be mistaken, reflect biases, and produce convincing nonsense. And something made by humans can still be dangerous.

But the analogy invites a better question than simply asking whether AI is a menacing new intelligence.

**What does it mean to create something from our collective knowledge that can then reason with us about the world?**

Perhaps the first step is to stop treating it as something that emerged from nowhere. It emerged from human language, knowledge, creativity, and ingenuity—transformed by mathematics, engineering, and computation into something we can interact with.

## Required reading

If you haven't read *Truckers* by Terry Pratchett, I recommend it—not just to people working in technology, but to their families too. It is funny, accessible to older children, and full of questions about knowledge, belief, and how a civilisation understands itself.

Read it together. Then ask: **What would we make of the Thing if we were the Nomes? And what are we making of our own Thing today?**

*Truckers* (1989) is the first book in Terry Pratchett's *Bromeliad Trilogy*, followed by *Diggers* and *Wings*.

People are afraid of LLMs—even some of the people building them. There are real risks, but we need a better mental model of what large language models actually are.

In Terry Pratchett's *Truckers* (1989), tiny people called Nomes carry a mysterious black box, **the Thing**, handed down through generations. They treasure it without understanding it. For years, it remains silent.

Then they arrive at a department store. Near its electrical supply, the Thing comes to life and speaks. It reveals that their ancestors came from a civilisation capable of space travel, and that the box is a computer preserving knowledge of their forgotten past.

But the Thing does more than tell them who they are. It answers their questions, helps them solve problems and guides them through unfamiliar challenges. What seemed like an alien intelligence is their own civilisation's knowledge speaking back—and helping them.

LLMs can feel just as mysterious. But their intelligence wasn't simply programmed into a clever algorithm. The breakthrough was finding a way to learn from vast amounts of information—and discovering that **the more these models learned at scale, the more capable they became**.

Much of that information came from the internet: writing, ideas, explanations and discoveries contributed by ordinary people from all walks of life. The models don't literally store all that knowledge, but they learn patterns from it and can apply them to our questions and problems.

**The knowledge created by people from all walks of life is the foundation of what makes LLMs intelligent. They let us interact with patterns of our collective intelligence. They are cultural artifacts, not alien visitors.**

That doesn't erase the risks. It changes how we understand them.

**My recommendation:** read *Truckers* with your family, especially your kids. Then consider our own Thing—and embrace it as a cultural artifact, something of ourselves to understand and use thoughtfully.
